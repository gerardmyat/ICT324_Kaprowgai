Will It Get a Season 2?

Predicting Television Anime Sequel Production Using Classification & Association Rule Mining

Myat Htoo Kaung 6708426 Aung Kabar 6708217 Aphisith Vongxay 6708522

Rangsit University International College • Academic Year 2026

Research Question

"To what extent can supervised machine learning and association rule mining accurately predict whether a first-season television anime receives a verified sequel within three years, based on public audience engagement metrics and production metadata?"

The Problem

Most television anime end after one season — even popular ones. Sequel decisions are made by private production committees (seisaku iinkai) composed of publishers, broadcasters, and distributors using internal financial data that outsiders cannot access.

Streaming platforms, licensors, and media analysts currently have no empirical, reproducible framework to estimate whether a show will continue. Existing anime data mining research focuses on predicting ratings, forecasting genre trends, and building recommendation systems — but sequel prediction remains unaddressed as a classification problem.

This matters because critical acclaim and commercial continuation frequently diverge: a beloved original series may end in one season due to exhausted source material, while a mid-rated manga adaptation with steady viewer retention receives multiple sequels.

Our Approach

We frame sequel production as a binary classification task — did the anime receive a verified sequel within 3 years of its first season? (has_sequel = 1 or 0). The study combines two complementary tracks:

Supervised Classification

Four models — Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost — evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation and SMOTE for class imbalance handling.

Association Rule Mining

Apriori and FP-Growth algorithms to discover interpretable multi-attribute patterns that co-occur with sequel continuation or single-season termination.

We follow the CRISP-DM six-phase methodology and use SHAP values for global and local feature-level explainability.
